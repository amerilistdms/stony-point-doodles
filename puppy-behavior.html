Puppy Behavior and Good Manners

Raising a great dog starts with the habits you teach today.

The foundation

Consistency is everything

Puppies learn through repetition. When everyone in the household responds the same way to the same behaviors, your Multigenerational Mini‑Bernedoodle learns faster and with less confusion.

What feels adorable at 10 pounds may not feel adorable at 50, 60, or 70 pounds. Ask yourself: Will I still think this is cute when my Bernedoodle weighs 50, 60, or 70 pounds? If the answer is no, do not encourage the behavior now.

Good manners are built one small decision at a time. The work you put in during the first few months pays off for years.

Normal puppy stuff

Nipping, biting, and chewing

Puppies explore the world with their mouths. Nipping and chewing are normal, but they still need guidance.

When your puppy nips, stay calm. Redirect to an appropriate chew toy. If the nipping continues, end the interaction briefly so your puppy learns that biting ends the fun.

Provide plenty of approved chews and rotate toys to keep things interesting. Supervise closely and puppy proof your home so your Multigenerational Mini‑Bernedoodle is not learning to chew shoes, cords, or furniture.

Rest matters

Energy versus biting

Sometimes what looks like aggressive biting is actually an overtired puppy. Young dogs need a surprising amount of sleep. A tired puppy can turn into what some families call a land shark.

If nipping spikes after playtime or in the evening, try a quiet break in the crate or a pen with a chew. A nap often resets the mood better than more stimulation.

Greetings

Jumping

Puppies jump because they are excited and want attention. The best response is to withhold attention until all four paws are on the floor.

Ask visitors to ignore your puppy until calm, then greet gently. Teach an alternative behavior, like sitting, for greetings. Be consistent. Mixed messages slow progress.

Communication

Understanding growling

Growling is communication, not defiance. A puppy may growl when startled, uncomfortable, or guarding something they value.

Do not punish growling. Punishment can suppress warning signals and make behavior harder to read. Instead, identify what triggered the growl and address the underlying cause.

If growling happens around food or toys, see the section on trading below. If growling is frequent, intense, or escalating, consult a qualified trainer or your veterinarian.

Resource guarding

Food, toys, and trading Drop It

Some puppies growl over food bowls, chews, or stolen items. This is common and manageable when handled thoughtfully.

Teach Drop It by trading: offer something better in exchange for what your puppy has. Practice with low value items first, then build up. Never grab items from your puppy's mouth forcefully.

Feed meals in a calm area. Let your puppy eat without hovering or reaching into the bowl during early training. If guarding is severe, seek professional help early.

Start young

Grooming manners early

Handle paws, ears, mouth, and body in short, positive sessions from day one. Pair handling with treats so grooming feels normal, not scary.

Puppies who learn to accept brushing, nail checks, and gentle restraint at home are easier for groomers and veterinarians to work with as adults.

Boundaries

House rules now

Decide your rules before your puppy arrives and stick to them. Will your dog be allowed on furniture? Sleep in the bed? Greet people at the door freely?

There is no single right answer, but inconsistency creates confusion. Agree as a household and follow through every day.

Rules about counters, trash, and personal space should be clear from the start. It is much easier to teach good habits early than to undo bad ones later.

Family life

Puppies and children

Puppies and children can be wonderful together with supervision and clear expectations. Teach children to be gentle, respect the puppy's space, and leave the dog alone while eating or resting.

Never leave a young puppy alone with small children. Even a sweet puppy can nip or become overwhelmed. Adults should manage interactions and step in when either side needs a break.

Short sessions

Training basics

Keep training sessions short and positive. A few minutes several times a day beats one long session that loses your puppy's attention.

Focus on these foundations:

  • Name: Reward your puppy for looking at you when you say their name.
  • Sit: A default behavior for greetings, meals, and calm moments.
  • Come: Practice in low distraction settings first, always making it worth their while.
  • Down: Useful for settling in busy environments.
  • Stay: Start with one second, then build duration gradually.
  • Off: For jumping on people or furniture when that is not allowed.
  • Leave It: Helps prevent grabbing dangerous or unwanted items.
  • Drop It: Essential for trading and safe toy play.
  • Calm: Reward quiet, relaxed behavior so your puppy learns that calm gets attention too.

Use high value treats, praise, and play. End on a success so your puppy looks forward to the next session.

The long view

Patience

Puppies make mistakes. So do people. Progress is rarely linear. Some days will feel easy, and others will test your patience.

Stay consistent, take breaks when you need them, and remember that your Multigenerational Mini‑Bernedoodle is learning a whole new world. The habits you build now shape the dog your family will live with for many years.
